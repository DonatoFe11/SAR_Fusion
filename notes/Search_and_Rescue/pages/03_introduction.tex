\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Wilderness Search and Rescue (SAR) requires locating missing or injured people
over large and visually complex areas while time, personnel, visibility, and
accessibility are constrained. Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) can reduce the
area that must be inspected from the ground and provide a viewpoint that is
otherwise difficult or dangerous to obtain. Turning aerial imagery into a
reliable detection signal is nevertheless challenging: a person may occupy
only a small fraction of the frame, vegetation and terrain create clutter, and
appearance changes substantially with illumination, altitude, season, and
weather.

Visible and thermal infrared cameras provide complementary information. The
visible stream contains texture, colour, and scene context, whereas the thermal
stream responds to emitted radiation and can retain a useful human signature
when visible contrast is weak. WiSARD was introduced specifically to support
the study of visual--thermal perception for wilderness SAR across varied
acquisition conditions~\cite{broyles2022wisard}. Complementarity, however,
does not imply that using both streams is automatically beneficial. The two
cameras are physically distinct, their fields of view and effective scales may
differ, and their projections are not perfectly registered. Moreover, either
stream may be absent or uninformative in practice. A multimodal detector must
therefore learn not only how to combine two signals, but also when and how much
to rely on each one.

These deployment concerns interact with an experimental one. The datasets and
training budgets available for UAV-based RGB--thermal detection are modest in
comparison with general-purpose detection corpora. Small changes in
initialization, batch order, stochastic modality masking, or nondeterministic
GPU kernels can then lead to visibly different final scores. If architectures
are compared through a single execution, the resulting ranking may describe a
particular training trajectory rather than a repeatable architectural effect.

\section{Problem statement}

This thesis investigates person detection from paired RGB and thermal imagery
on the WiSARD dataset. The main detector family is RT-DETR, adapted to a
four-channel input through two independent backbones and feature-level fusion.
The baseline sums corresponding RGB and infrared feature maps at the P3, P4,
and P5 pyramid levels. The studied alternative transforms the infrared feature
map with a Feature Alignment Module (FAM) before the same additive fusion.

The FAM uses RGB and IR features jointly to predict deformable-convolution
offsets and modulation masks. This creates a spatially adaptive transformation
of the infrared representation, but the module is not supervised by geometric
registration labels. A higher detection score is consequently insufficient to
claim that physical sensor registration has been learned. Establishing what the
module does requires inspecting its internal variables and intervening on its
levels, in addition to measuring the detector output.

The study addresses four connected forms of robustness:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item \textbf{modality robustness}, when RGB or IR is unavailable;
  \item \textbf{geometric robustness}, when paired streams are imperfectly
        aligned or differ in scale;
  \item \textbf{experimental robustness}, when training is repeated under
        different seeds and nondeterministic execution;
  \item \textbf{architectural robustness}, when the same fusion idea is moved
        to another detector family.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research questions}

The thesis is organized around the following questions.

\paragraph{RQ1 --- Experimental reliability.}
How do run-to-run variability, nondeterministic GPU operations, validation
mismatch, checkpoint selection, and process isolation affect the reliability
of RGB--IR detector comparisons?

\paragraph{RQ2 --- RT-DETR effectiveness and missing modalities.}
Under a locked paired-seed protocol, does FAM provide a consistent advantage
over Additive fusion in RT-DETR, and how does it affect performance when one
input modality is unavailable?

\paragraph{RQ3 --- Mechanism and failure modes.}
What geometric-functional transformation does FAM learn across P3, P4, and P5,
how stable is it across seeds and sessions, and which pyramid levels contribute
to detection performance? Can a smooth bounded-offset constraint remove an
observed internal collapse without reducing detection quality?

\paragraph{RQ4 --- Operational relevance.}
How does FAM change the detector's error profile, its behavior under severe
sensor-scale mismatch, and its computational cost?

\paragraph{RQ5 --- Architectural transferability.}
Do the benefits observed with RT-DETR transfer to other detector families under
comparable evaluation rules?

\section{Scope and relationship with preceding coursework}

This thesis continues two preceding course projects. The Deep Learning project
studied DETR, Modal Dropout, RT-DETR Additive fusion, fixed tiling, and CMX. The
Computer Vision project subsequently introduced the FAM on RT-DETR, designed a
Hybrid CMX variant, and adapted Deformable DETR to channel fusion with FAM.
These components define the starting point of the present work.

The FAM is therefore \emph{not} presented as a new architecture invented in
this thesis. It is described in full so that the document remains
self-contained, but its provenance is stated explicitly. The present
contribution is the reliability-aware validation of that module: its
performance across paired runs, its internal behavior, its failure modes, the
effect of a targeted correction, and the extent to which the result transfers
outside the original RT-DETR setting.

The distinction also applies to the evidence. Early experiments were useful
for discovering bugs and generating hypotheses, but many were individual runs
produced before the final validation and checkpoint policy was defined. They
are retained as an exploratory development history and are not mixed with the
main multi-seed estimates.

\section{Contributions}

The contributions developed after the preceding course projects are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item an audit of variability, validation composition, checkpoint policy,
        model initialization, GPU determinism, and process isolation;
  \item a locked RT-DETR comparison of six configurations over five paired
        seeds, including initialization and direct-warp ablations;
  \item a multi-seed diagnostic study of offsets, masks, feature statistics,
        PCA projections, and session dependence;
  \item a factorial post-hoc ablation of FAM activity at P3, P4, and P5;
  \item the identification of a compensated P5 collapse and a five-seed test
        of a smooth bounded-offset correction;
  \item a paired error analysis across confidence thresholds, including
        size-stratified recall and predefined qualitative examples;
  \item a frozen one-shot stress test on the Carnation acquisition and a
        controlled detector-only compute benchmark;
  \item a complete DINO implementation with Contrastive DeNoising, Mixed Query
        Selection, and Look-Forward-Twice, reported as a negative exploratory
        result for the studied configuration;
  \item an extension of the Deformable DETR line with FAM and Stochastic
        Spatial Jitter, retained as exploratory evidence;
  \item a dual-backbone YOLOv10 implementation with Additive or FAM fusion and
        feature-level modality gating, evaluated under a five-seed final
        protocol.
\end{enumerate}

Negative results are part of these contributions. They delimit which
mechanistic changes improve internal behavior without improving detection and
which RT-DETR findings fail to transfer under the studied conditions.

\section{Thesis organization}

\Cref{chap:background} introduces the dataset, detector families, multimodal
fusion, missing-modality training, and the inherited architectural starting
point. \Cref{chap:methodology} reconstructs the post-coursework exploratory
development and explains how the variability investigation led to the final
protocol. \Cref{chap:evaluation} presents the main RT-DETR results, internal

boundaries, and outlines future work.
