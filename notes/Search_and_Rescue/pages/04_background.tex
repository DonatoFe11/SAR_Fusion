\chapter{Background and Research Starting Point}
\label{chap:background}

\section{UAV-based RGB--thermal detection for Search and Rescue}

Person detection in aerial wilderness images differs from conventional
street-level pedestrian detection. Subjects are frequently small, partially
occluded, and observed from changing altitudes and viewing angles. Natural
backgrounds provide repeated textures, warm rocks may resemble thermal human
signatures, and targets may exhibit weak visible or thermal contrast. The
WiSARD dataset addresses this setting with labeled visual and thermal images
collected from UAV flights over diverse terrains and acquisition conditions~\cite{broyles2022wisard}.

RGB--thermal sensing seeks to reduce dependence on a single observation
channel. Multispectral pedestrian benchmarks have long shown the potential of
combining visible and thermal cues~\cite{hwang2015multispectral}, while the
broader multimodal-learning literature emphasizes both complementarity and the
difficulty of learning balanced representations~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal,wang2020multimodal}.
In this thesis, robustness is evaluated explicitly in three input conditions:
full fusion (VIS+IR), visible-only (VIS), and infrared-only (IR).

\section{Object detection and evaluation}

An object detector maps an image to a set of class scores and bounding boxes.
For a predicted box \(B_p\) and a ground-truth box \(B_g\), Intersection over
Union is

\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=
  \frac{|B_p\cap B_g|}{|B_p\cup B_g|}.
\end{equation}

Average Precision (AP) integrates precision as a function of recall after
ranking detections by confidence. The primary metric in the final experiments
is \mapfifty{}, for which a match requires IoU at least 0.50. COCO mAP averages
AP over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 and provides a stricter localization
summary~\cite{lin2014coco}. mAP@75 and mean Average Recall (mAR) are retained as
secondary metrics.

The confidence value used to collect detections is not itself the IoU match
criterion. A low confidence threshold preserves candidates for AP computation,
but may be unusable as an operational alarm threshold. This distinction is
important in \Cref{sec:error-analysis}, where threshold 0.01 produces high
recall and tens of false positives per image.

\section{Detection Transformer families}

\subsection{Transformer encoder--decoder and attention}

The Transformer was originally introduced as an encoder--decoder architecture
built around attention rather than recurrent processing~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.
For a set of query, key, and value vectors, scaled dot-product attention is

\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)=
  \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
  \label{eq:scaled-dot-product-attention}
\end{equation}

where \(d_k\) is the key dimension. The dot products measure which values are
relevant to each query, while the softmax produces normalized aggregation
weights. Multi-head attention repeats this operation in several learned
subspaces and combines their outputs, allowing different heads to represent
different relationships.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{images/transformer_architecture_vaswani.png}
  \caption{The original Transformer encoder--decoder architecture. The encoder
  (left) alternates multi-head self-attention and feed-forward sublayers. The
  autoregressive decoder (right) adds masked self-attention and cross-attention
  over the encoder output. Residual connections and normalization surround
  every sublayer, while positional encodings retain sequence order. Source:
  Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.}
  \label{fig:original-transformer-architecture}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:original-transformer-architecture} shows the original
sequence-to-sequence model. Its encoder builds contextual representations of
the complete input sequence, while the autoregressive decoder combines its
previous output tokens with the encoder representation through cross-attention.
The original model therefore uses a causal mask in decoder self-attention, while
vision-detection variants modify this setup according to their prediction task.

\subsection{DETR}

DETR applies this architecture to direct set prediction~\cite{carion2020detr}.
After the backbone and a channel projection, the original model flattens a
single feature map into a sequence and passes it through the Transformer
encoder. A fixed number of learned object queries enters the decoder, and each
query produces either one object prediction or the special no-object class.
The output size is therefore fixed, while the number of real objects can vary
between images.

Unlike the original sequence-to-sequence Transformer, DETR replaces input-word
embeddings with image features and shifted output tokens with object queries, while
class and box heads replace the vocabulary softmax. Image tokens are augmented
with positional encodings to retain their two-dimensional locations. The object
queries are decoded in parallel, so DETR does not use an autoregressive causal
mask. Decoder self-attention models relations among candidate objects, whereas
cross-attention retrieves relevant evidence from the encoded image features.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/detr_original.png}
  \caption{DETR architecture. A CNN backbone produces image features that are
  combined with positional encodings, contextualized by a Transformer encoder,
  and decoded from learned object queries into class and bounding-box
  predictions. Reproduced from Fig.~2 of Carion et al.~\cite{carion2020detr}.}
  \label{fig:detr-architecture}
\end{figure}

Training must decide which predicted query corresponds to which ground-truth
object. DETR solves this with one-to-one bipartite matching: the Hungarian
algorithm selects the assignment with minimum classification and localization
cost, after which matched pairs receive classification and box-regression
losses and unmatched queries are trained as no-object. Because every target is
assigned to only one query, duplicate predictions are discouraged by the
training objective itself. The original inference pipeline consequently does
not require handcrafted anchors or non-maximum suppression.

This simplification has costs. Dense encoder self-attention compares all
spatial tokens, so its memory and computation grow quadratically with feature
resolution. The use of a relatively coarse, single-scale feature map also
limits the detail available for small objects. Moreover, one-to-one matching
is initially unstable because object queries do not yet specialize, leading to
the slow convergence reported for the original DETR. These limitations
motivate the later variants used or explored in this study.

In the work that preceded this thesis DETR was used to establish the
value of stochastic missing-modality training. DETR is not rerun in the final
campaign, its role is to motivate the training regime inherited by later
architectures.

\subsection{Deformable DETR}

Deformable DETR replaces dense attention over all spatial locations with
multi-scale deformable attention around learned reference
points~\cite{zhu2021deformable}. Instead of comparing every query with every
feature location, each attention head predicts a small number of sampling
offsets around a reference point at each feature level. Values at fractional
coordinates are obtained by bilinear interpolation and combined through
learned attention weights. The sampling cost therefore depends on the small
number of selected points rather than on all spatial cells.

The model consumes a feature pyramid rather than only the deepest backbone
map. High-resolution levels retain spatial detail for small targets, while
lower-resolution levels provide wider context. Reference points also give each
query an explicit spatial anchor, and iterative box refinement can update this
reference as it passes through decoder layers. Together, sparse sampling,
multi-scale features, and spatial references improve convergence and are
particularly relevant to aerial images, where a person may occupy very few
pixels.

The configuration used in this thesis provides four feature levels to the
detector. The three native ResNet-50 outputs, \(C3\), \(C4\), and \(C5\), are
fused across RGB and IR; a fourth, coarser level is derived from the fused
\(C5\) feature by stride-2 projection. FAM operates only at the three native
levels before the fourth is formed.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth,trim=0 0.72in 0 0,clip]{images/deformable_detr_original.png}
  \caption{Deformable DETR uses multi-scale deformable self-attention in the
  encoder and deformable cross-attention in the decoder. Each attention module
  samples a sparse set of learned locations instead of attending densely over
  every feature-map cell. Reproduced from Fig.~1 of Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2021deformable}.}
  \label{fig:deformable-detr-architecture}
\end{figure}

Deformable attention should not be confused with the deformable convolution
used by the FAM. The former is an attention rule that samples multi-scale
features for each query, while the latter transforms a dense IR feature map before
fusion. They share the idea of learned sampling offsets but act at different
places in the detector and have different parameters and outputs.

\subsection{DINO}

DINO builds on the multi-scale deformable DETR line and targets two remaining
problems: unstable matching early in training and the quality of decoder query
initialization~\cite{zhang2023dino}. Contrastive DeNoising (CDN) adds noisy
copies of known labels and boxes during training and asks the decoder to
reconstruct them. Positive and harder negative denoising queries teach the
model how small perturbations should affect classification and localization,
providing a more direct learning signal than Hungarian matching alone at the
start of training.

Mixed Query Selection (MQS) initializes the positional part of decoder queries
from selected encoder features while retaining learned content queries. This
separates the questions of \emph{where} a candidate object may be and
\emph{what} it represents. Look-Forward-Twice (LFT) strengthens iterative box
refinement by allowing the parameters involved in one refinement step to
receive supervision from the following decoder layer as well. These mechanisms
are complementary: CDN stabilizes training queries, MQS improves their spatial
initialization, and LFT improves the learning signal across refinement stages.
A complete implementation was developed and is documented separately in
\Cref{sec:dino-exploration}.
It retains the same four-level multi-scale representation as the Deformable
DETR adaptation.

\subsection{RT-DETR}

RT-DETR retains end-to-end set prediction while redesigning the multi-scale
encoder for lower latency~\cite{zhao2024rtdetr}. Its efficient hybrid encoder
separates two operations that a standard multi-scale Transformer would perform
together. Attention-based intra-scale feature interaction is applied only to
the deepest, lowest-resolution feature level, where global semantic
interaction is comparatively inexpensive. A convolutional cross-scale feature
fusion module then propagates information through the feature pyramid. This
division reduces the cost of applying self-attention densely at several
resolutions.

The decoder is initialized through uncertainty-minimal query selection. The
encoder scores candidate features and selects high-quality spatial locations
for the initial queries, seeking candidates whose classification and
localization estimates are reliable. Decoder layers then refine these
queries into the final detection set. Since the decoder is the repeated part
of the detection head, its number of layers can also be adjusted to trade
accuracy for latency without retraining the encoder.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/rtdetr_original.png}
  \caption{RT-DETR architecture. Its efficient hybrid encoder combines
  attention-based intra-scale interaction with convolutional cross-scale
  fusion, while IoU-aware query selection initializes the Transformer decoder~\cite{zhao2024rtdetr}.}
  \label{fig:rtdetr-architecture}
\end{figure}

The implementation used here starts from the pretrained RT-DETR R50vd
checkpoint and exposes three backbone outputs, denoted P3, P4, and P5 with
strides 8, 16, and 32. The dual-backbone adaptation preserves the downstream
hybrid encoder and decoder: the experimental variable is how corresponding RGB
and IR features are transformed and fused before entering them.

RT-DETR is designed for real-time object detection. In this thesis, computational
efficiency is characterized through detector-only forward-pass latency measured
on a specified GPU. Preprocessing and postprocessing are excluded, so these
measurements compare the computational cost of the detector configurations
rather than establish end-to-end real-time deployment performance.

\subsection{YOLOv10}

YOLOv10 is an end-to-end member of the YOLO family that combines consistent
dual assignments with an efficiency-oriented convolutional
architecture~\cite{wang2024yolov10}. As in other one-stage YOLO detectors, a
backbone extracts a feature pyramid, a neck combines fine spatial detail with
coarser semantic information, and a dense head predicts classes and boxes at
multiple locations, which is structurally different from decoding a small fixed
set of object queries.

Traditional dense detectors can produce several boxes for the same object and
therefore rely on non-maximum suppression. YOLOv10 instead trains two
assignment branches consistently: a one-to-many branch supplies abundant
positive matches for optimization, while a one-to-one branch learns the
non-redundant predictions used for end-to-end inference. The matching rules are
coordinated so that the richer supervision of the first branch benefits the
second rather than teaching incompatible rankings. At inference time, the
one-to-one output removes the need for NMS.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{images/yolov10_dual_assignment_original.png}
  \caption{YOLOv10 consistent dual assignments. A shared backbone and PAN
  support one-to-many and one-to-one detection heads during training; only the
  one-to-one branch is retained for NMS-free inference.~\cite{wang2024yolov10}.}
  \label{fig:yolov10-dual-assignments}
\end{figure}

The thesis adapts YOLOv10-s to two modality-specific backbones and fuses their
P3, P4, and P5 outputs before the shared FPN/PAN neck. It therefore provides a
useful transfer target: both detector families receive corresponding
multi-scale RGB and IR features, but RT-DETR processes them with a hybrid
encoder and query decoder, whereas YOLOv10 processes them through a
convolutional neck and dense detection head.

\section{Multimodal fusion}

Fusion methods can be grouped by where the modalities interact. Early fusion
combines inputs or low-level representations, late fusion combines independent
predictions, and intermediate fusion combines learned features. This work uses
intermediate fusion because each sensor receives a dedicated backbone while
the detector still operates on a joint multi-scale representation.

The inherited RT-DETR baseline uses element-wise addition:

\begin{equation}
  F_{\mathrm{fused}}^{(i)} = F_{\mathrm{RGB}}^{(i)} + F_{\mathrm{IR}}^{(i)},
  \qquad i\in\{P3,P4,P5\}.
  \label{eq:additive-fusion}
\end{equation}

Addition introduces no trainable fusion parameters and preserves feature-map
shape, but assumes that corresponding cells are meaningfully compatible. A
sensor displacement or scale difference violates this assumption. More complex
interaction does not automatically solve the problem: multimodal networks can
overfit or optimize their modalities at different rates~\cite{wang2020multimodal}.

Previous work also examined CMX, a cross-modal rectification and
fusion framework originally proposed for RGB-X semantic
segmentation~\cite{zhang2023cmx}. The specific RT-DETR integration was not
competitive on WiSARD. This finding is retained as an exploratory result of
that implementation, not as a general rejection of attention-based fusion.

\section{Missing modalities and Modal Dropout}

Modal Dropout was proposed to preserve modality-specific representations by randomly
dropping input channels during multimodal training~\cite{neverova2016moddrop}.
This study applies the same principle at the modality level. For a paired
sample \((x_{\mathrm{RGB}},x_{\mathrm{IR}})\), training draws one of three
conditions:

\begin{equation}
  \widetilde{x}=
  \begin{cases}
    (x_{\mathrm{RGB}},0), & \text{with probability }p_{\mathrm{VIS}},\\
    (0,x_{\mathrm{IR}}), & \text{with probability }p_{\mathrm{IR}},\\
    (x_{\mathrm{RGB}},x_{\mathrm{IR}}), & \text{with probability }p_{\mathrm{fusion}}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:modal-dropout}
\end{equation}

The inherited schedule uses
\(p_{\mathrm{VIS}}=0.2\), \(p_{\mathrm{IR}}=0.2\), and
\(p_{\mathrm{fusion}}=0.6\). It is a motivated exploratory choice from the
preceding DETR study, not a statistically demonstrated optimum. The equation
specifies which sensor is present, but not its coordinate contract. In the
RT-DETR data loader used here, an IR-only draw uses the native IR canvas and
native IR annotations. This distinction becomes important in
\Cref{sec:coordinate-contracts}. A software audit conducted for this thesis
also found that modality dropout was being applied during validation and test.
The pipeline was corrected so that the sampling in
\Cref{eq:modal-dropout} is active only during training.

\section{Feature-level alignment and fusion}

\subsection{Deformable convolution in the FAM}

This subsection introduces DCNv2, the operation used by the Feature Alignment
Module (FAM) described next to transform IR feature maps before fusion. It is
distinct from the multi-scale deformable attention used inside Deformable DETR:
DCNv2 is a convolutional operation on a dense feature map, whereas deformable
attention samples encoder or decoder features for a query.

Standard convolution samples a fixed grid \(\mathcal{R}\) around each spatial
position \(p\). Modulated deformable convolution augments every kernel point
\(p_k\) with an input-dependent offset \(\Delta p_k\) and a modulation value
\(m_k\)~\cite{zhu2019dcnv2}:

\begin{equation}
  y(p)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{R}}
  w_k\,x(p+p_k+\Delta p_k)\,\sigma(m_k).
  \label{eq:dcnv2}
\end{equation}

Sampling at non-integer positions uses bilinear interpolation. The operation
therefore combines a geometric sampling change with learned filtering and
channel mixing. Its output cannot be interpreted as a pure warp unless those
additional effects are controlled.

\subsection{Inherited Feature Alignment Module}

For every pyramid level, the FAM concatenates RGB and IR features and predicts
18 offset channels and 9 mask logits for a \(3\times3\) DCNv2 kernel:

\begin{align}
  [\Delta,m] &=
  \operatorname{Conv}_{3\times3}
  \left([F_{\mathrm{RGB}},F_{\mathrm{IR}}]\right),\\
  \widetilde{F}_{\mathrm{IR}} &=
  \operatorname{DCNv2}(F_{\mathrm{IR}},\Delta,\sigma(m)),\\
  F_{\mathrm{fused}} &= F_{\mathrm{RGB}}+
  \widetilde{F}_{\mathrm{IR}}.
  \label{eq:fam-fusion}
\end{align}

The offset/mask predictor is initialized to zero, so initial offsets are null
and masks are approximately 0.5. The DCNv2 filter weights retain their own
initialization. Consequently, the standard FAM does \emph{not} start as a
mathematical identity: null offsets preserve the initial sampling geometry but
do not make the filtered output equal to the input.

Because there is no ground-truth displacement field, the thesis uses the term
\emph{geometric-functional transformation}. Nonzero offsets show that the
geometric path is active, while they do not prove recovery of the physical mapping
between the cameras. This distinction motivates the internal diagnostics in
\Cref{sec:fam-diagnostics}.

\section{Dataset organization and evaluation conditions}

All final comparisons use synchronized WiSARD RGB--IR pairs. The effective
split audit is summarized in \Cref{tab:dataset-splits}. Earlier
experiments also used unpaired visible or thermal folders to vary the training
inventory. The final study instead restricts training and primary evaluation to
paired samples, so that a modality comparison does not also change the set of
scenes or annotations.

The pre-existing 273-frame validation set contains one FHL session, 184 empty
frames, and 148 boxes. Its median target area is approximately twelve times
smaller than in training. It is informative as evidence of a strong
distribution shift, but it is not a representative selector for performance on
the three MtErie sequences. This is distinct from the 896-frame complete-video
validation introduced for Stage A.

MtErie denotes the three synchronized WiSARD VIS--IR sequences used for the
main 708-frame benchmark. Their VIS annotations define the common reference
coordinate system and ground truth for the final comparisons. MtErie was
consulted during architectural development and is therefore an \emph{internal
development benchmark}. Final paired comparisons remain useful, particularly
after the protocol was frozen, but may be optimistic with respect to a new acquisition.
Carnation denotes a separate paired WiSARD acquisition used once as a predefined
739-frame external stress case: its visible camera is substantially more zoomed-in
than its thermal camera, creating a severe cross-modal scale mismatch. It is not a new
validation set or an estimate of general domain generalization.

At evaluation time, the three modality conditions retain a common dataset and
annotation set:

\begin{description}
  \item[VIS+IR] both streams from each synchronized pair are supplied;
  \item[VIS] the same paired frame and VIS annotations are retained, but its
        thermal input is masked, so no IR signal is available to the model;
  \item[IR] the same paired frame and VIS annotations are retained, but its
        visible input is masked, so no RGB signal is available to the model.
\end{description}

Thus, VIS and IR are controlled missing-input evaluations rather than datasets
in which the opposite sensor file is absent. RT-DETR represents the missing
stream as zero-filled input while retaining its four-channel interface; the
YOLOv10 evaluator uses an explicit modality mask and bypasses the excluded
backbone and FAM path. The conditions therefore share images and annotations,
but their internal execution is not architecturally identical.

\section{Inherited experimental starting point}

Previous work established DETR with Modal Dropout, the RT-DETR Additive
baseline, a fixed \(2\times2\) tiling experiment related to slicing-aided
small-object detection~\cite{akyon2022sahi}, and the initial CMX comparison.
It subsequently introduced FAM on RT-DETR, Hybrid CMX, and Deformable DETR
with nonlinear channel fusion and FAM. The resulting historical scores were
based primarily on the earlier checkpoint policy and, for several RT-DETR
variants, on individual executions.

Those results generated the central hypothesis that transforming IR features
before fusion could be preferable to direct addition. They did not yet answer
whether the advantage was consistent across runs, whether the learned offsets
were geometrically meaningful, whether all feature levels behaved correctly,
or whether the result transferred to another detector. These questions define
the point at which the present thesis begins.
